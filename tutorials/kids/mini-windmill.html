How to Build a Mini Windmill (Safely)

For parents and teachers.

⚠ Safety notes

  • Use blunt tools only. Kids get safety scissors; adults handle anything sharper.
  • Adults do all cutting, poking holes, and hot gluing.
  • Never plug this project into a wall outlet. The only power here comes from wind (or your breath).
  • Keep small parts like LEDs, wire bits, and the motor away from kids under 3. They're choking hazards.
  • Skewers are pointy. An adult should snip the tip off or cover it with a bead or a blob of tape.
  • Don't stand right in front of a fast fan with your face close to the blades.

A Batch & Build STEM project for ages 8 to 12

Wind is invisible, but it's strong enough to push sailboats, flip umbrellas inside out, and power whole towns. Today your child can catch some of it with a windmill they build from stuff you probably already have.

Grown-up note

This project needs an adult helper. Kids can do most of the building, but an adult should handle every cut, every poke with a sharp point, and any hot glue. Plan on about 45 minutes.

What you'll need

For the windmill

  • 1 sturdy paper cup (the base)
  • 1 piece of cardboard about 4 inches square, or a second paper cup (for the blades)
  • 2 drinking straws (one wide, one that fits loosely inside it, or one straw plus the skewer)
  • 1 wooden skewer, tip snipped off by an adult
  • Tape (masking or clear)
  • A small lump of modeling clay or a few coins (to weigh down the base)
  • A ruler and a pencil
  • Safety scissors

Optional power add-on

  • 1 small DC hobby motor (the kind from science kits)
  • 1 small LED
  • 2 short wires with alligator clips

To test it

  • A desk fan, a hair dryer on the cool setting, or a breezy day

Let's build it

  1. Weigh the base. Put the clay or coins inside the paper cup, then flip it upside down. Now it won't tip over when the wind hits.
  2. Make the tower. Tape one straw standing straight up on top of the upside-down cup. This is your windmill's tower.
  3. Add the axle holder. Cut the second straw to about 3 inches long with safety scissors, then tape it sideways across the very top of the tower, like the top of a letter T. The skewer will spin inside this straw.
  4. Draw your blades. On the cardboard square, draw an X from corner to corner. Make a dot in the center.
  5. Cut the blades (adult job). An adult cuts along each line of the X, stopping about half an inch from the center dot. You'll have four flaps still joined in the middle.
  6. Bend the blades. Fold one corner of each flap toward the center and tape it down, always folding the same side. It should look like a pinwheel. (Using a cup instead? An adult cuts the cup into 6 to 8 strips down to the bottom, and you bend each strip out at the same angle.)
  7. Poke the hub (adult job). An adult pushes the blunt end of the skewer through the center dot. Tape around it on both sides so the blades and skewer turn together.
  8. Put it together. Slide the skewer through the sideways straw. Leave a little space between the blades and the straw so nothing rubs.
  9. Lock it in place. Wrap a small band of tape around the back end of the skewer so it can't slide out.
  10. Test it! Point a desk fan, or a hair dryer on the COOL setting only, at the blades from about two feet away. Never let the fan or dryer touch the blades. Watch them spin.

Bonus: make it light up

  1. An adult tapes the motor to the top of the tower so its little shaft points forward.
  2. Instead of a skewer, press the blades' center firmly onto the motor shaft. Add a dab of clay or tape so they grip.
  3. Clip one wire from each motor tab to each leg of the LED.
  4. Turn on the fan, the faster the better. If the LED doesn't glow, swap the two wires on the LED. LEDs only work one way around.

Heads up: a small hobby motor and one LED often won't light from a desk fan, because the blades don't spin fast enough. To check your wiring, give the blades a quick, fast spin with your hand. If the LED blinks, your circuit works and you just need more wind.

A tiny LED may only flicker, and that's still a win. Your young builder just turned wind into electricity.

The science: how wind becomes power

The sun heats the Earth unevenly. Warm air rises, cooler air rushes in to take its place, and that moving air is wind.

Moving things carry energy, called kinetic energy. When wind hits your slanted blades, it pushes them sideways, and that push makes them spin. The spinning skewer is now carrying the wind's energy.

With the motor attached, something extra happens. Inside the motor are magnets and coils of wire. Spinning a coil near a magnet makes electricity flow through the wire. That's why the LED lights up. Real wind turbines work the same way, just with blades longer than a school bus.

Troubleshooting

  • Blades won't spin. Make sure every blade is bent the same direction, and check that nothing is rubbing against the straw.
  • It spins slowly or wobbles. Push the blades closer to the center of the skewer and make sure they're the same size.
  • The whole thing tips over. Add more clay or coins to the base.
  • The skewer slides out. Add another tape band behind the straw.
  • LED won't light. Swap the LED wires, move the fan closer, or spin the blades fast by hand to test the circuit. Small motors need a lot of speed.

Take it further

  • Blade challenge. Try 2, 4, 6, and 8 blades. Which spins fastest?
  • Angle test. Bend the blades a little and then a lot. What angle works best?
  • Wind meter. Put a sticker on one blade and count how many times it passes the top in 10 seconds at low, medium, and high fan speeds.
  • Do some lifting. Tie a thread to the back of the skewer with a paper clip on the end. Can your windmill reel it up?
  • Design your own. Try different materials like paper plates, foil, or plastic cups and see which makes the best blades.

Nice work. Together you just built a power plant on your desk.
